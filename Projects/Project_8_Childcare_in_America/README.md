# Childcare in America: The Rising Cost of Care

## Overview
This project (DSC 640 – Data Presentation & Visualization) analyzes the U.S. Department of Labor's **National Database of Childcare Prices (NDCP)**, which tracks weekly childcare prices across every U.S. county from 2008 to 2018. The project's core deliverable is a three-part communication strategy — a slide deck for policymakers, an interactive dashboard for analysts, and a written narrative for the general public — each targeting a different audience with the same underlying data.

## Dataset
The National Database of Childcare Prices, compiled by ICF for the U.S. DOL Women's Bureau, containing county-level weekly median childcare prices (50th percentile) for center-based and family/home-based care, standardized to weekly full-time rates, spanning all 50 states and D.C.

## Approach
The project was built as three parallel communication mediums from a single analysis:
1. **Medium 1 (PowerPoint)** — high-level charts and slide-formatted takeaways aimed at state policymakers.
2. **Medium 2 (Interactive Dashboard)** — drill-down visualizations aimed at analysts who want to explore the data themselves.
3. **Medium 3 (Written Narrative)** — an infographic-style write-up for general public readers, especially working parents, using large "big number" statistics, plain-language explanations, and a journalistic inverted-pyramid structure.

## Key Findings
- **Center-based infant care rose ~26%** in real dollars between 2008 and 2018, while median household incomes grew only ~13% over the same period — the affordability gap widened every year.
- **Geography matters enormously**: weekly infant care costs range from ~$500 in D.C. to ~$158 in Arkansas — a gap of over $340/week, or roughly $17,000/year.
- **Family/home-based care is consistently cheaper** than center-based care by $66–$80/week across all age groups, though both have followed the same upward trajectory.
- **The burden falls unevenly**: single mothers can spend upward of 57% of income on childcare for one infant in a high-cost state, far above the U.S. HHS affordability threshold of 7% of family income.
- Even in lower-cost states, families often spend a larger *share* of income on childcare because local wages are also lower — sticker price alone doesn't capture affordability.

## Policy Implications
The write-up highlights four levers commonly cited by researchers and advocates: expanding federal/state childcare subsidies (CCDBG currently reaches fewer than 1 in 6 eligible children), investing in the childcare workforce (median wage ~$13/hour), incentivizing employer-sponsored childcare benefits, and funding universal Pre-K programs.
