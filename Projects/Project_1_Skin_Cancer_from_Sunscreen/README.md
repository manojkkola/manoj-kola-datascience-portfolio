# Sunscreen vs. Skin Cancer: A Risk-Benefit Analysis

## Overview
This project investigates a question that sits at the intersection of public health messaging and consumer safety: **does sunscreen genuinely protect against skin cancer, or could some of its chemical ingredients pose a cancer risk of their own?**

Sunscreen is universally recommended by dermatologists and health agencies as a first line of defense against skin cancers like melanoma, basal cell carcinoma (BCC), and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). At the same time, a growing body of research has raised questions about whether common chemical UV filters — oxybenzone, octinoxate, and homosalate among them — might act as endocrine disruptors or carry their own long-term health risks. This project weighs those two sides of the story against each other.

## Research Question
Does the risk of harm from chemical sunscreen ingredients outweigh the well-documented protective benefit sunscreen provides against skin cancer?

## Approach
The analysis was built in three milestone stages:

1. **Milestone 1 — Proposal & Initial Research** — framed the core question and surveyed existing evidence on sunscreen chemical absorption, skin cancer incidence, and public sentiment via Google Trends.
2. **Milestone 2 — Deeper Analysis & Layered Questions** — expanded the investigation with a structured set of research questions covering which skin cancers sunscreen prevents, which chemicals are under scientific scrutiny (oxybenzone, octinoxate, homosalate, titanium dioxide, zinc oxide), and what concentrations are considered harmful.
3. **Milestone 3 — Final Synthesis** — consolidated the research into a complete comparative risk analysis with conclusions and recommendations.

Two companion presentations summarize the project for general and technical audiences.

## Key Findings
- Skin cancer, especially melanoma, remains a well-documented and serious disease — the American Cancer Society reports over 97,000 new melanoma diagnoses and roughly 8,000 deaths annually in the U.S.
- Some chemical sunscreen filters (e.g., oxybenzone) have been detected in the bloodstream at levels above FDA safety thresholds, but current evidence does **not** establish that they cause cancer in humans — the data is suggestive, not conclusive.
- Public concern (tracked via Google Trends search data) has risen in step with media coverage, even though the underlying science is still developing.
- On balance, sunscreen's well-established protective benefit against skin cancer currently outweighs the more speculative and unproven risks tied to its chemical ingredients — though mineral-based alternatives (zinc oxide, titanium dioxide) offer a lower-uncertainty option for concerned users.

## Ethical Considerations
- **Equity of access**: sun protection recommendations should account for socioeconomic barriers and differences in occupational or cultural sun exposure.
- **Responsible communication**: raising legitimate questions about chemical absorption must be balanced against the risk of discouraging sunscreen use altogether and inadvertently increasing skin cancer risk.
- **Informed choice over fear**: the goal of this work is to support evidence-based decision-making, not alarmism.
